\subsection{Study of Existing Systems}

Before building anything, it is worth asking what already exists. Four
categories of tool could plausibly be pointed at this problem. None of them
solves it, but the reasons they fail are specific rather than general, and those
reasons are what shaped the design in Section~1.5.

\subsubsection{Manual consultation and document search}

The current process is a person opening the PDF and using whatever search their
viewer provides. Full-text search finds character strings. That helps when you
already know the exact title of an activity or its identifier, and it helps very
little otherwise. Searching for ``approve'' returns hundreds of hits across the
document with no ranking by relevance. Searching for a half-remembered activity
name returns nothing unless the words you remember match the words that were
printed.

There is a deeper limit. Search returns locations, not answers. It can tell you
that a phrase appears on page 24. It cannot tell you who approves the activity
described there, because doing so would require knowing that the page holds a
table, that the table's headers are rotated, and that a particular letter in a
particular cell means approval by a particular role.

\subsubsection{Generic document question-answering assistants}

A second category is the general-purpose document assistant: upload a PDF, ask
questions in plain language, get answers back. Tools in this space include
ChatPDF, NotebookLM, Microsoft Copilot and the file-upload modes of the major
chat assistants. They work by splitting a document into passages, embedding
those passages as vectors, retrieving the passages closest to the question, and
handing them to a language model that writes the reply.

On prose this works well. On this document it fails, and the reason is
structural rather than a question of model quality. Converting a DAM page to
plain text destroys the spatial relationships that carry its meaning. A rotated
header that identified a column becomes a loose string somewhere in the
extracted text, no longer attached to the codes beneath it. A retrieved passage
can therefore contain an activity name and the letter A without preserving the
fact that the A sat in the column belonging to a specific director. The model is
handed text from which the answer has already been removed, and it produces
something plausible from what is left.

\subsubsection{Commercial document-extraction services}

A third category does not answer questions at all, but extracts structure:
Azure Document Intelligence, AWS Textract, Google Document AI, and more recent
layout-aware parsers such as LlamaParse. These are considerably better at tables
than a general-purpose library, and they are the serious alternative to writing
extraction rules by hand.

Three things ruled them out here. They are priced per page and require sending
an internal governance document to an external service, which is a decision a
bank makes deliberately rather than one a student makes casually. They are tuned
for common business documents, invoices, receipts, contracts, forms, rather than
for a bespoke authority matrix with rotated headers and superscript-qualified
codes. And when they get a cell wrong they offer no account of why, which
matters in a document where a wrong answer looks exactly like a right one. A
rule derived from a measured character offset can be checked against the page
that motivated it. A confidence score cannot.

\subsubsection{Critical analysis: limitations of current approaches}

None of these approaches fails through lack of engineering. Each fails because
of an assumption that does not hold for this document.

Text search assumes the user can phrase their need in the document's own
vocabulary. Generic assistants assume that flattening a document into passages
preserves its meaning, which is false for a table whose semantics are
positional. Extraction services assume the document resembles the business forms
they were trained on. Enterprise governance platforms, the fourth category, do
model delegation of authority properly, but they expect the institution to
re-enter its rules into their own data model, which is a change-management
programme rather than a tool.

There is also a limitation the first three share, and it matters more than any
of the above. None of them has a concept of being wrong. A search returns
results, a model returns text, and in neither case does the system distinguish
an answer it can support from one it cannot. For a compliance reference, that
distinction is the entire point. Table~\ref{tab:existing} sets the four
